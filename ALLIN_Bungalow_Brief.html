Betting on America

What Is Polymarket

Polymarket is a prediction market. Anyone with a crypto wallet can bet real money on real-world outcomes: elections, military strikes, government decisions, the death of a head of state. It is not a stock exchange and not technically a casino, which is how it has avoided most regulation. It was founded in 2020 by Shane Coplan, who was 22 years old at the time, and is registered in Panama.

The concept is old. Prediction markets have existed in academic and limited commercial forms since the 1990s. The theory: people betting real money on an outcome produce more accurate forecasts than any poll or model. The problem: the same mechanism that makes them accurate makes them a tool for anyone who already knows the answer.

History of the Ban

In 2022, the CFTC fined Polymarket $1.4 million for operating an unregistered derivatives exchange and required it to stop serving US users. Polymarket did not shut down. It moved offshore to Panama and kept building. A US user with a VPN and a crypto wallet can access it in under five minutes. The ban accomplished one thing: it put the platform outside US regulatory reach while doing almost nothing to stop American participation.

Kalshi is the US-registered version, a CFTC-supervised prediction market that launched after the offshore model proved the demand. Kalshi operates under US rules. Polymarket does not. The serious money and the risks all live offshore.

The Worst Case Scenario

What is already happening: US military personnel with advance knowledge of operations are placing bets before those operations execute, then collecting when they succeed. A US Army intelligence officer was federally indicted for doing this on the Venezuela operation. Campaign staffers admitted anonymously to betting on their own candidates using internal polling data. Nobody has been charged for that.

What could happen next: Polymarket odds are broadcast in real time on CNN and Reuters. When a cluster of wallets bets heavily that a US strike will occur in the next 48 hours, every adversary monitoring the platform sees that signal. The element of surprise is gone before the planes leave the runway.

The question nobody wants to say out loud: if you have the ability to influence an event, and a financial stake in a specific outcome, you have both means and motive to make it happen. DARPA identified this in 2003 and killed its own program because of it. The same concept is now running commercially, offshore, with hundreds of millions of dollars in bets on US government actions every month.

The DARPA Moment

In 2003, DARPA proposed a prediction market called the Policy Analysis Market to gather intelligence on Middle East political instability. Senators Ron Wyden and Byron Dorgan took the Senate floor and called it a "terrorism futures market," asking one question: what happens if a terrorist bets on the attack he is planning? DARPA canceled the program within 48 hours.

The concept did not die. It moved offshore, evolved through two decades of academic research and crypto infrastructure, and became Polymarket. Nobody has asked the same question from the Senate floor this time.

The Iran Example

Before a US military strike on Iran, Polymarket odds shifted sharply. A cluster of wallets with no prior trading history placed large bets on the strike when the market considered it unlikely. When the strike happened, those wallets collected. Data scientist Michelle Kendler-Kretsch had been tracking the market in real time, running probability models in her notebook, watching the odds move. She was awake at 4am with her one-year-old daughter when the news confirmed what she had been watching on her screen.

"The element of surprise is everything. And you've ruined that on Polymarket?"

Michelle Kendler-Kretsch  ·  Data Scientist  ·  60 Minutes

Elections

Prediction markets now cover 96% of US primary races. The same platform that broadcast the Iran strike in advance runs markets on every congressional seat heading into the midterms. A single $1,000 bet on a candidate triggers a cascade of automated bots that follow large money moves, making a candidate appear more likely to win without any actual shift in voter support. None of that counts toward campaign finance limits. Nothing stops a foreign government from doing it.

Think of a Polymarket bet as a campaign contribution with a bot multiplier. You invest a small amount that looks like a large amount, and none of it falls under any donation cap. If the money comes from overseas, there is currently no mechanism to stop it or trace it.

Romania

In 2024, $371 million was wagered on Romania's presidential election on Polymarket. The figure dwarfed the country's entire political advertising market. Romania banned Polymarket outright. One expert told me on the record, for the first time publicly, that Romania got it right and the markets should be banned. That is her personal position, separate from her organization's official stance. She made that distinction explicitly when she spoke to me.

Money Laundering

In Ireland's 2024 Dublin Central election, more than $1 million was wagered on Polymarket. Researchers found that 86% of the trading volume was wash trading: money moving between wallets controlled by the same actor to simulate market activity. The Irish Times investigated and surfaced the name Gerry Hutch, a figure with documented connections to organized crime. The investigation is ongoing. No charges have been filed. Nobody knows yet if this was Polymarket being used as a laundering vehicle or one bad actor finding a gap. Either answer is a problem.

What the Experts Are Saying

The Facebook parallel keeps coming up. Zuckerberg built a connection platform. The outcome in Myanmar was genocide, because he connected people who wanted to find each other for that purpose. The technology outran the capacity to regulate it. Prediction markets are the same dynamic applied to war, elections, and government decisions, with one difference: prediction markets are not an accidental side effect. They are specifically designed to put money on these outcomes.

"These markets touch democracy, they touch national security."

How It Works: Click by Click

  1. Someone with insider knowledge places a bet through an anonymous crypto wallet on an offshore platform the US government cannot touch.
  2. The blockchain is public. Bots and sophisticated traders monitor it in real time, see the unusual movement, and pile on.
  3. The market shifts, broadcasting the insider signal to everyone watching, including foreign adversaries.
  4. When the event resolves, the insider collects. The bots collect. The platform takes its fee. The government learns about it months later, through a journalist or an indictment.
  5. Nobody goes to jail. The CFTC has one commissioner instead of five. Enforcement dropped from 58 cases in 2024 to 13 in 2025. The wallets are anonymous. The platform is offshore. There is no explicit statute making any of this illegal. December 7 will be the first time a US court has to answer that question.

Four More Things

Selling positions. You do not have to wait for an event to happen to profit. If you know a US strike is coming, you buy a "yes" position when the odds are 10% and the price is cheap. As insiders and bots move the market, the price of your position rises. You sell at 60% before the strike happens and pocket the gain. The event never has to occur. This is how insider trading works in equity markets, applied to military operations.

Market manipulation. The price on Polymarket is set purely by bettors, not by any external model. One large bet moves the price. That movement triggers bots that move it further. This is by design. The theory is that money on the line produces accurate information. The problem is that it also makes the market trivially easy to manipulate. You do not need to know the outcome. You need capital and no conscience.

Are people incentivizing outcomes? Can those outcomes kill people? The Van Dyke indictment does not allege he manufactured the Maduro operation. He knew about it and bet on it. But the market structure creates a financial incentive to make specific events happen. At sufficient scale, with sufficient access to the right levers, that is not a theoretical concern. DARPA identified it in 2003 and killed its own program because of it. Nobody killed Polymarket. The experts I have spoken to believe the scenario is possible. None of them will say it has happened yet. None of them will rule it out.

Who decides what you can bet on? The platform does. Before 2000, the CFTC required pre-approval of every new derivatives contract. The Commodity Futures Modernization Act eliminated that and replaced it with self certification: platforms declare that their own contracts meet legal standards. Kalshi listed a contract on the death of Iran's Supreme Leader. It was removed after the fact. There is no process that says no before a market goes live.
