Pedro
What do you need people to understand about Polymarket? For the layperson who doesn't know anything.
Michael
Insider information is a feature on prediction markets. That's something people need to understand. People think of these as a way that the average member of the public has a fair shot at getting rich by essentially getting lucky. Like playing the lottery, a good bet on the stock market, getting into the right crypto venture at the right time. But all of these things are structurally designed to benefit other people. Insiders. People highly capitalized, highly professionalized. Whether it's the house at your casino in Vegas, Wall Street, the Trump family who own the crypto meme coin you're buying, or somebody in the government or the military on Polymarket. The people participating as the average person on the street are structurally set up to lose. You'd be mad to bet on these unless you really know what you're talking about or you have some kind of edge.
Michael
A lot of people do have that edge. We can't say for certain, but there's reason to believe that could be people in the military and the intelligence community. But it could also be somebody with a very expensive subscription to satellite live-tracking technology and imagery. They could look at a nuclear site. They might be at a space agency somewhere. They know something happened, the market hasn't moved yet, and they put their money on it. That's what we call information asymmetries. They're inherent to these markets. That's what makes them work.
Michael
My colleague Michelle, who wrote the report, was in Mexico during spring break and there was a kid in front of her putting money on the Iran bet. Insane, just on the face of it. But also, you can't monitor a situation closely enough to consistently win on these things. Studies show the concentration of profits on these markets is absolutely tiny. Something like 70% of profits going to 1% of accounts. Whether it's insiders or people who can see what insiders are doing and then copy those actions on the blockchain. If you're smart, you can set up bots to look for particular patterns. Copy those, throw thousands of dollars onto those bets because somebody seems to know what they're doing, and you make a lot of money.
Michael
On our next report, we look at how this happens in detail. Profit concentration among longshot bets. These are bets that shouldn't win that often, but they win a lot. Even within that, there's a tiny slice making all the money. They're basically tracking insiders and jumping on what they do. Think about someone loading bombs onto a plane on an aircraft carrier. That person knows what's up. Say they break the law and take the bet. They're certain something is happening, but how much cash do they have at their disposal? We set the threshold at $2,500 because if somebody is really confident, some people will have that sitting in an account. But then there are bets where people put on $10,000, $15,000, $20,000, mirroring those same activities. They're doing this across markets all the time. When they win, they win really big. Very small concentration of actually profitable winning traders, even among unlikely longshots.
Michael
It's rigged. I wouldn't say that on camera or in print. But people need to know these things are in various ways rigged. It's not a fair economic system. What gets very worrying is the military angle. National security implications. But it's also a lot of election trading. These are getting more and more concentrated. People are betting now on what the margin is going to be, what the turnout is going to be. There are tiny municipal elections in Eastern Europe with thousands and thousands of dollars bet on them. Obviously not by people from the town. We don't fully understand why. But it can move perceptions. These markets touch democracy, they touch national security.

Michael
That's the extreme case, but it's not impossible. More likely: if you want to do a stealth military attack, and the fact that it's about to happen is broadcast over Polymarket because the odds have moved and there are signs of an insider and then these big accounts start moving on something. If you're an adversary watching this and you think, people seem to think we're about to get hit tomorrow. From a US point of view, that's bad. That's not what should be happening. Whether or not people can manipulate real outcomes for their own profit, you'd have to be very high placed to have that power. But what we have with Van Dyke and those people: they were Pentagon. Pedro

Michael
Right. It adds perverse incentives to things that don't need additional perverse incentives. That got shut down because it was a more sane political time and people said, what are you talking about? But there's a parallel to what now exists with Polymarket. It's registered offshore in Panama. Not subject to US regulations. The offshore platform includes markets on military events. Even if you start excluding certain categories, there are chains of events and consequences. Take oil prices: if you're going to blow up a ship in the Suez Canal, you take the bet on oil prices, because you know there's going to be an impact there.

The Khamenei death bet and Rule 40.11
Pedro
Someone made $553,000 betting on the death of Iran's Supreme Leader. The contract shouldn't have existed under Rule 40.11. Why is that person not in a courtroom?
Luong
I won't give you a satisfying answer. I don't know why. It's unclear whether there is some internal investigation going on. Selig can't really share much when something is under investigation. But I don't know. That poses more questions about whether the structure is equipped to handle these kinds of suspicious trades or not. A lot of the responsibility is placed on the platform itself, which has an incentive not to react quickly. It has to go through that layer, then get to the CFTC to be investigated. That doesn't seem efficient. Kalshi or Polymarket is not going to say let's not make money. Their surveillance structure is a mix of human oversight and automated oversight. Not everything is done by hand. Before 2000, the CFTC used to pre-approve every single contract. Under the Commodity Futures Modernization Act passed around 2000, the discourse reflected very similarly to today: prediction markets have a lot of room for innovation. That was the justification for getting rid of pre-approval, because it was seen as burdensome. That is how this platform self-policing structure was born. As a consequence, you have less oversight. Going back to pre-approval makes logical sense to me, but we are understaffed, so how do we address that.
Campaign staffers and the legal gap
Pedro
Your May fact sheet said campaign staffers admitted to betting on their own candidates using internal polling data. Were any of them charged?
Luong
Not that I know of. They admitted it anonymously in public reporting. It is highly unlikely that they would penalize staff-level government employees. But it is a cause for concern, because once you open that box, it forces the conversation. It pretty much forces Congress to actually do something. I think a lot of people on the Hill are hesitant, especially Republicans. That is not a partisan statement I am making. But if you look at the bills that have been introduced, they are mainly supported and introduced by Democrats. However, there are some bipartisan bills and some Republican members have also introduced legislation. I don't think it is in Congress's best interest to do anything about it.
Luong
I'll also add that with prediction markets, whether there is even a case to charge someone based on misappropriating information, the legal guidance around what counts as insider trading for event contracts is much less clear than for securities. The Van Dyke case will be the first case to test how information gets transferred and what counts as transferring material nonpublic information for personal gain.
Van Dyke, wire fraud, and why not 40.11
Pedro
Why was Van Dyke charged with wire fraud and not with breaking Rule 40.11?
Luong
He had a crypto wallet and transferred the funds. I think wire fraud is the charge that would allow disgorgement of what he earned. That is my quick answer. Also, charging for fraud, the law is much clearer. The crime is new. There's no precedent for 40.11 applied against an individual. So they lean on what's already established, what has had precedent before. Applying laws we already know to prediction markets is something being tested right now: how much does the law governing other financial instruments also apply to event contracts. But also, charging under the special rule right now would be difficult because the language is overly broad. They could make a case it's not in the public's interest, but there is no ban on event contracts related to the military. Period. So to charge under 40.11 they would need explicit language that doesn't fully exist right now.
Whether a whole new framework is needed
Pedro
Does this mean we need a whole new framework, a whole new set of laws?
Luong
My first answer would be no. We don't have to create a whole new framework, legal or regulatory. There is already a weak structure we could build on top of. POGO has supported efforts that expand the kinds of event contracts that should be prohibited. We have supported banning government action contracts outright, because we don't think money should touch politics. That is a very hot take in America. On using prediction markets as campaign contributions, that's not my area of expertise. But from a democracy standpoint, it's very troubling. Because now you have voters with a financial incentive in mind rather than a moral or ethical one. Instead of voting for the candidate that's right for you, you're voting for the one that might be the more profitable option. That only makes government more inefficient.
Luong
On the event contracts with death: why was that created in the first place? And why, when we have the CFTC as our oversight structure, did they not act? The response time is really slow, and a lot of responsibility is placed on the platform itself, which has an incentive not to react quickly. I also think the economic purpose test, which was eliminated around 2000 under that same legislation, should be brought back and refined. It was a test that determined whether an event contract has a significant economic function. If that kind of process was more refined and improved, we probably would have answers to some of the questions we are asking now.
The information exchange and journalistic integrity
Luong
How I see a prediction market: you're in a big conference room, the size of the market, with a large group of people exchanging information but throwing money into it. Information exchange is a natural part of life. When you start putting money into it, the social stakes are higher. That changes how we view the world and how we value things. Also: prediction markets have been relatively quiet until 2024 and we've done fine. We have journalists, we have credible resources that exist and wouldn't induce these social and political consequences. I don't know if you've heard about traders threatening journalists. That is real. Journalistic integrity is on the line. Sure, this could be a great way to get information, but at what cost? We have structures in place. It just depends on what society values.
Insider trading as a feature of these markets
Pedro
It seems like all these markets function with someone having insider information. Someone knows something. One guy places a $1,000 bet. Someone bigger picks up on it, puts in $10,000. The bots find it and amplify. So there is always going to be one percent of people winning and the majority losing. Is that true? Is that how you see it?
Luong
It's more nuanced than a yes or no, because that is how other financial markets also work with securities and stocks. The difference is that cracking down on insider trading and fraud is much more regular in securities markets than in event contract markets. I don't think that's what makes event contracts unique. That is a natural component of these kinds of financial markets. Technically you could say that, but I don't think that's the real issue. The real issue is the lack of regulations and laws governing them. Now if that is the case, it's an argument for whether we should just ban all of these markets, because it's so hard to pinpoint who knew what and how they came to their position. If you see a large bet on an option with low odds, that seems suspicious. But it's not necessarily breaking the law. It's a happy coincidence. It's really hard to catch how information gets transferred, especially when trading is done through an intermediary. So yes, it's more nuanced than a simple yes.
