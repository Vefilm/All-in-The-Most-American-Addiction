05, DARPA
"Congress killed it in 24 hours. Twenty three years later, it came back private."
The DARPA Precedent

Congress killed this idea in 24 hours. Twenty three years later, the private sector built it anyway.

2003
DARPA kills it
2024
Private sector rebuilds it

DARPA, the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency, is the Pentagon division that invented the internet. In 2003, through its Information Awareness Office, the same unit simultaneously proposing mass surveillance of all American digital communications, DARPA created the Policy Analysis Market. The premise: allow intelligence analysts and the public to trade futures contracts on geopolitical events, assassinations, coups, and terrorist attacks. The logic being that if an insider knew a strike was coming and placed a bet, the price signal would surface the threat before it happened. The program was overseen by John Poindexter, the figure at the center of the Iran Contra scandal, who had returned to government after 9/11 to build Total Information Awareness, the most ambitious domestic surveillance architecture in American history.

Senators Byron Dorgan and Ron Wyden held a press conference on July 28, 2003. Dorgan called it "useless, offensive and unbelievably stupid." Wyden called it "a federal betting parlor on atrocities and terrorism." Within 24 hours, the Pentagon cancelled the Policy Analysis Market. Congress called the plan "morally repugnant." Poindexter resigned within the week. By September, Congress had defunded the entire Information Awareness Office.

Polymarket's main exchange runs offshore, outside US rules, with markets on military strikes, presidential actions and geopolitical outcomes. After the Justice Department and the CFTC closed their investigations in 2025, it bought a small CFTC licensed exchange and came back to the US. More than thirty countries have moved to restrict it, including Romania, France, Belgium and Ireland. The CFTC under Michael Selig brought 13 enforcement actions in 2025, down from 96 two years earlier. The idea Congress killed in 24 hours is now a private business valued at $21 billion, and the president's son advises both of the biggest platforms.

Episode Structure

The series has the shape of a thriller and the argument of an essay.

EP 1
The Addiction
Cold Open
The hangar. The phone. The bet already placed.
We open on the machine working, before the audience knows what the machine is. The airman sequence, the Polymarket UI glowing, the money moving. Then: the phone in the evidence bag. Then: the investigators discovering the pattern. The question that drives the series is established: how is this legal?
EP 2
The Machine
The Inciting Question
How did a $1.4M fine become a $21B company?
The machine's architecture: three tiers of advantage, the DARPA precedent, the ownership sequence. Kovel names the legal gap. Hornsby maps the structure. The ACDC data team shows the wallets, the Orcas and the whales that copy them. The Congo parallel establishes the frame: this has happened before, in a different suit.
EP 3
The Owners
The Escalation
The investigators take it to Congress. The trial approaches. The clock ticks.
Warren's letter. The CFTC's silence. The Chicago enforcement office resignations. Every door the investigators knock on. The Van Dyke trial as ticking clock, not the series' resolution, but the last moment of public visibility before the window closes. Janice Luong at POGO on what good legislation would look like versus what's actually moving.
EP 4
The Reckoning
The Reckoning
The data is published. The wallets are still active. Nobody acted.
The Citizenfour ending. The investigators have shown everything, receipts on the blockchain, structural parallels in published research, a whistleblower lawyer naming the crime on camera. The machine is still running. That's the series. Not a conviction. Not a resolution. A reckoning.
On the Trial: USA v. Van Dyke (Dec 7, 2026) is the ticking clock in Episode 3, not the load bearing climax. If the date slips, the series' argument doesn't change. The machine runs with or without the verdict. If the trial concludes during production, it becomes one more data point in the reckoning. If it doesn't, the Warren silence and the still active wallets close the series instead.

Scenes We Will Shoot

Six specific sequences. This is what the film looks like.

Scene
01
The Wall of Screens, Washington
Michelle Kendler-Kretsch at her screens. A brand new wallet places an outrageous bet on a military market. Within minutes the whales and bots pile in behind it. She traces the chain back to the first bet and narrows the pool of who could have known. Real time. Repeatable. The moment where invisible becomes visible.
Scene
02
Kovel, The Trading Floor Memory
David Kovel, sitting across from us, describing what a suspicious chart feels like from the inside, before any numbers are run. He was a trader before he was a lawyer. He knows the tell. We hear his description while we see the on chain data on screen: the same tell, a generation later, in a different market. The cut that connects them is the argument.
Scene
03
The Recovery Room, Verite
A sports betting addiction group session. The participants describe what the app feels like, the pull notification, the near miss, the next deposit. The phone, close up. The number going down. This is the front door, the thing the platforms want you to see, and the thing that hides the building behind it. Shot on a longer lens. Observational. No narration.
Scene
04
Truth Predict on a Phone, Stylized
A phone screen, in close up, in a dark room. The Truth Predict interface, the prediction market tool built into the MAGA ecosystem. Someone is placing a bet on a government action. The bet will win. The phone is the antagonist. This is the stylized version of the machine: the tool, the action, the profit. No face. No name. No allegation. Just the act.
Scene
05
Courthouse Steps, Van Dyke Trial
No cameras inside. We're outside. The investigators arriving. Reporters waiting. Court sketches of the testimony. Then: actors in a neutral space, reading the transcript verbatim, the actual words from the record, spoken, unadorned. This is the gold standard for covering a trial without cameras. Accurate. Legal. More cinematic than reconstruction.
Scene
06
Hornsby, The Structural Parallel
Michael Hornsby, in Europe. He pulls up the Congo Hold-Up network diagram on one screen, the Polymarket investor map on another. He traces the nodes: head of state, family insider, captured regulator, anonymous vehicles. "Same structure. Different suit." This is the film's intellectual moment. The argument that makes everything else land.

Pedro Feria Pino, SOC is a New York based Director of Photography, Director, and Camera Operator with over 15 years of experience working across documentary, television, and branded content. A member of the Society of Camera Operators, his work is defined by a cinematic approach grounded in observation, trust, and story.

Pedro began his career in Puerto Rico as a stringer cameraman for Reuters News, developing the instincts and discipline of visual journalism. He went on to create Ego Moda, the first fashion focused television program in Puerto Rico, showcasing emerging designers and photographers while establishing his voice as a visual storyteller. In 2010, he moved to New York City, where he cocreated, produced, and served as camera operator on Washington Heights for MTV, the first Latino centric reality series on the network. Premiering in 2013, the show brought authentic Latino stories into the mainstream and stands as a landmark in Latino representation on American television.

His cinematography career has taken him from the jungles of Guatemala documenting Mayan rituals to underground boxing gyms, ballroom competitions, and intimate veríte environments across the world. He has served as Director of Photography on productions for Netflix, Disney, AMC, National Geographic, MTV, VH1, and ABC, including Buried by the Bernards, Ride with Norman Reedus, Generation X, Strange Medicine, and Los Montaners. As a director and supervising producer, his credits include House of Cardi, Everybody Loves Natti, and Love and Hip Hop Miami.

He has also operated camera on Comedians in Cars Getting Coffee, Indian Matchmaker, and Red Table Talk. He is based in New York and works worldwide.
